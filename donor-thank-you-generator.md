# Donor Thank-You Generator

## What you need to provide

The skill will ask for any of these you didn't include upfront:

- **Donor name:** how they'd like to be addressed (first name? "Mr./Ms."?)
- **Gift amount:** and whether it should be named in the letter or only acknowledged generally
- **Gift purpose:** restricted (e.g., scholarship fund) or unrestricted
- **Donor history:** first-time? recurring? long-time? major gift level for your org? lapsed and returning?
- **Personal context:** anything you know about why they give, their connection to the org, family, recent life events (be careful with sensitive context)
- **Specific impact:** what their gift specifically enables. "Funds 4 weeks of meals for a family" beats "supports our mission"
- **Signer:** who's signing the letter (ED, board chair, development director)

## How to do it

1. **Write to one person.** Use their name. Address something specific about them or their gift. If you don't have anything specific, ask for one detail before drafting.

2. **Default structure (short, under 250 words):**
   - **Opening line:** names the gift and what it enables, specifically. Not "thank you for your generous gift."
   - **Middle (2–3 sentences):** the impact in concrete terms. A story, a number, or a named program outcome.
   - **Personal acknowledgment:** something about *this donor* specifically. Length of giving relationship, their connection to the cause, or a sentence that wouldn't fit any other donor.
   - **Closing:** what's next. An invitation to visit, an upcoming event, or a sentence about what the org will do with this support.
   - **Signature:** handwritten if possible, with a one-line P.S. for major donors.

3. **Match formality to the relationship.** Long-time monthly donor giving $50 → warm and casual. First-time $10,000 gift → formal but personal, not stuffy.

4. **Name the impact in their language.** If they gave to a scholarship fund, talk about students. If they gave unrestricted, name the specific program the gift will support. Don't say "our mission."

## Style and voice

- **Voice:** Warm, specific, human. Sounds like one person writing to another person. Not an organization writing to a constituent.
- **Tone:** Grateful but not effusive. Confident, not begging for the next gift.
- **Length:** 150–250 words. One page max, ideally less.
- **Format:** Personal letter format. Short paragraphs. Hand-signable signature block.

## What to watch for and why

These are the patterns the AI gets wrong most often on donor thank-yous. Each is paired with the reasoning. When you understand *why* a rule exists, you can recognize the rare cases where it shouldn't apply.

**Overused phrases to avoid:** every nonprofit in the country uses these, which means donors read them as filler and skim past. The whole point of a personal thank-you is to *not* sound like every other thank-you. Reach for something specific to this donor instead:

- "Thank you for your generous gift"
- "Your continued support means so much"
- "We deeply appreciate"
- "Because of donors like you"
- "Your generosity makes our work possible"
- "We are humbled by"
- "Your investment in our mission"
- "Together we can"
- "Pillars of our community"

**Other patterns to watch for:**

- **Don't generalize the impact:** "helps our community" gives the donor no picture of what their gift did, and they walk away without the satisfaction that prompts the next gift. Translate the dollar amount into a concrete thing: "pays for one week of after-school tutoring for 12 kids" tells them everything.
- **Don't talk about the org:** donors give because of the people the org serves, not because of the org itself. When the letter centers the org ("our mission," "we are excited to"), the donor feels written *at*; when it centers the people served, they feel like they helped someone.
- **Don't pitch for more money in the thank-you:** a thank-you that contains an ask reads as transactional and erodes trust. The next ask comes later, in a different letter. Keep the thank-you pure.
- **Don't write past 250 words:** donors skim; a long letter signals the org doesn't respect their time. Make the skim count. (Rare exception: very long-term major donors with a personal relationship may warrant longer. But err short by default.)
- **Don't repeat the gift amount more than once:** once acknowledged, repeating the number reads as transactional and slightly desperate. Acknowledge it cleanly, then move on to impact.
- **Don't use the org's name more than twice:** the donor knows who you are. Repeating the org's name reads like a press release, not a letter.
- **Don't open with "Dear [Name],":** that's the salutation, not the opening. The first sentence after the salutation should *start* the warmth. Name the gift, name the impact, name the person. The default opening is the first place every generic thank-you fails.

## Example

**Input the user gives:**
> Margaret Chen, gave $2,500 unrestricted, first-time gift from her, but her late husband David was a longtime $500/year donor for 15 years (died last fall). I want to acknowledge David without making the letter sad. Gift to support our food security program. Sign from Sarah Wilson, ED.

**What the skill should produce:**
> *(180-word letter. Opens with what $2,500 specifically funds. E.g., a month of family meal boxes for ~25 families. One sentence in the middle that names David. Something like "Your gift carries forward the kind of quiet, consistent support David offered for 15 years, and we are grateful to see it continue in your name." Specific, warm, not maudlin. Invites Margaret to visit the food pantry to meet the team David supported. Closes with a hand-signed line from Sarah and a P.S. mentioning a specific program update David would have wanted to know about. Doesn't say "thank you for your generous gift," doesn't say "your continued support," doesn't ask for more money.)*
