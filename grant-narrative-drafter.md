# Grant Narrative Drafter

## What you need to provide

The skill will ask for any of these you didn't include upfront. The more you provide, the less generic the draft:

- **Program / project description:** who it serves, what activities, where, how often, by whom
- **The funder:** name, and ideally a paste of their priorities, mission, or RFP language
- **The ask:** dollar amount, project period (e.g., 12 months), what the funds will pay for
- **Word or character count:** and which sections it covers (whole narrative? one section?)
- **Required sections:** if the funder specifies sections (Need, Approach, Outcomes, etc.), paste them
- **Real numbers and stats:** outputs from last year, people served, % of population, anything verifiable
- **Distinctive context:** what makes this program different from a similar one in another city

## How to do it

1. **Mirror the funder's language, don't copy it.** If their priorities say "early childhood literacy," use "early childhood literacy," not "young learner reading skills." But never paste their text into the narrative verbatim.

2. **Lead with the population, not the organization.** Open with who is affected and what the gap is. The org shows up in the *Approach* section, not the *Need* section.

3. **Default structure (override if funder specifies different):**
   - **Statement of Need:** the problem in this community, with specific numbers
   - **Project Description:** what you'll do, who's involved, where, when
   - **Goals and Outcomes:** measurable results, ideally with baselines and targets
   - **Methods / Approach:** *how* you'll do it, briefly. Why this approach over alternatives
   - **Evaluation:** how you'll know it worked
   - **Sustainability:** what happens after the grant period (only if requested)
   - **Budget Narrative:** only if requested; otherwise leave for budget document

4. **Write in active voice.** "We will train 40 volunteers" not "40 volunteers will be trained."

5. **Hit the word count.** If the funder says 1,000 words, produce 950–1,000, not 600 and not 1,400.

## Style and voice

- **Voice:** Direct, confident, plain. No nonprofit jargon ("stakeholders," "leverage," "robust"). No buzzwords. Specific verbs and specific numbers beat adjectives every time.
- **Tone:** Earnest but not pleading. The funder is a peer making a smart bet, not a savior being begged.
- **Length:** Hit the funder's word/character limit precisely.
- **Format:** Headers for each section. Short paragraphs (3–5 sentences). Lists only when the funder uses them.

## What to watch for and why

These are the patterns the AI gets wrong most often on grant narratives. Each is paired with the reasoning. When you understand *why* a rule exists, you can recognize the rare cases where it shouldn't apply.

- **Don't invent statistics:** fabricated numbers are the fastest way to lose a funder's trust permanently, and the program team can't verify what you didn't ask for. If the user didn't provide a number, ask for it or write "[insert most recent count]" as a placeholder for them to fill in.
- **Don't open with the org's history:** funders fund the *gap*, not the org. Opening with the org signals you think they're funding you for your sake. Open with the problem in the community; the org's track record goes in the *Approach* section where it answers "why us."
- **Avoid "we are committed to," "passionate about," "deeply believe":** these phrases are invisible to funders because every applicant uses them. Show commitment through specific things you do, not through how strongly you feel.
- **Don't pad to hit word count:** funders read past the filler and the draft loses force. If the draft is short, ask the user for more specifics (real numbers, named outcomes) rather than adding adjectives.
- **Don't promise outcomes you can't measure:** vague outcomes like "increased community resilience" sound impressive but commit you to nothing the funder can hold you to, which makes the whole narrative feel unserious. If an outcome doesn't have a way to know whether it happened, sharpen it or cut it.
- **Don't copy the funder's RFP language verbatim:** funders can tell, and it reads like you didn't think. Mirror their priorities (use their terms like "K-12 education" if that's their language) but rewrite the sentences in your own words.
- **Avoid the word "innovative" unless the program genuinely is novel:** it's so overused it signals the opposite. If the program is unusual, describe *what* makes it unusual instead of using the label.

## Example

**Input the user gives:**
> We run a free after-school tutoring program in two elementary schools in Casper. Serving 60 kids in grades 2-5, mostly free/reduced lunch. Last year 85% of participants moved up a reading level. We're applying to the Daniels Fund. Priorities are K-12 education and serving rural/underserved kids. Asking for $40,000 over 12 months to add a third school. 800 words for the narrative.

**What the skill should produce:**
> *(800-word draft with Need / Project Description / Goals & Outcomes / Approach / Evaluation sections, mirroring "K-12 education" and "underserved" language from Daniels Fund priorities, using the 85% statistic provided, asking for the third school expansion specifically, in active voice with no buzzwords. Closes by inviting questions, not by thanking the funder for their consideration.)*
